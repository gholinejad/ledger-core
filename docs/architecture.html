In-Memory Account Ledger Core: Architecture and Trade-offs

Matt Gholinejad · Staff Software Engineer take-home · repository: ledger-core (Python, standard library only)

The core keeps three append-only logs: ledger entries, interest accrual memos and authorization transitions. Money is stored as integer minor units. A back-valued event never edits the past. Instead, every end-of-day close re-evaluates each day of the window as it is now known and posts only the difference, as FEE/FEE_REVERSAL entries or accrual deltas. Stream order is processing order, so a late event inside the window is posted on the day it arrives and flagged. Everything below follows from those choices.

1. Append-only at scale

What breaks first at 100× volume

The end-of-day close breaks first, before memory or the write path. In the current design it costs

accounts × days-in-window × entries

for these reasons:

  • balance(account, day) scans the whole entry list, including other accounts' entries.
  • For every account and every day back to Day 1, the close runs three balance() calls (two to restate the day, one for the report), plus a fee scan and an accrual scan.

Both the number of entries and the number of days grow with history, so the close is quadratic in history and also multiplied by the number of accounts. At 100× volume the close no longer fits its batch window long before posting slows down. This is the same failure pattern as a nightly settlement job that drifts from 1 hour to 4: the per-record cost isn't the problem, the growth curve is.

Cheapest structural change

Per-account daily balance snapshots plus a "dirty-from" low-water mark.
  • Keep, for each account, the closing balance per value day.
  • When an entry posts with value day v, set dirty_from[account] = min(dirty_from, v).
  • The close re-tests only dirty accounts, and only from dirty_from forward. Each day's balance is the previous snapshot plus that day's entries.

The cost drops to accounts (today only) + dirty account-days since each back-value. The entry log stays the source of truth: snapshots are a derived cache and can always be rebuilt by replay. Indexing entries by account is a one-line change that comes with it.

This defers the problem but does not remove it. A back-value to Day 1 still re-walks every day. The structural fix is a sealed period. After a period close, no entry may carry a value date inside it. A correction posts on the current date with a reference to the original, so the restatement horizon becomes bounded by policy, not by history. At that point the log can also be partitioned by account and by period, and sealed periods can move to cold storage with a checkpoint balance. The received set becomes an idempotency store with a TTL that matches the scheme's maximum retry window. I built that pattern for Colourlab AI's admin money actions (refunds, credit adjustments, grants). The key is unique per operator and key. A replay returns the first result, and the same key with a different payload gets a typed 409.

2. Value-dated entries in production

A value date lets an entry change a day that has already closed. In the replay, one back-valued debit (E7) caused fees on three days, zeroed three days of interest and caused a card decline. A reversal one day later then had to undo the fees and interest, but could not undo the decline. In a UAE-licensed bank each of those effects reaches a different owner:

  • Customer fairness and conduct. Under CBUAE consumer-protection rules, fees must be fair and disclosed, and charges that turn out to be wrong must be refunded. A back-value can create an overdraft fee for a day on which the customer actually had funds. The fee-reversal logic in this core is that refund. It must also produce a customer-visible correction, because a statement already issued, such as Day 5 at −410.00, cannot be recalled.
  • Interest and profit. Back-valuation moves interest between the bank and the customer, which is a direct P&L effect. For Islamic accounts it moves profit distribution, which is subject to Sharia board oversight.
  • Regulatory and financial reporting. Prudential returns to the CBUAE and the GL period close are taken as of a date. A value date inside a period that has already been reported changes a figure that has already been submitted, which means resubmission or a documented adjustment.
  • AML and audit trail. Back-dating is a known way to disguise the order of transactions. Investigators and the UAE FIU need both timelines, value date and booking date, and the identity of whoever back-dated. The same principle drove Triton's alert-ingest contract: every event carries both the device time and the broker receipt time, because I measured a device clock running 89 minutes fast. The UAE AML law requires transaction records to be retained for at least five years.
  • Operations. Every back-value creates reconciliation breaks against scheme settlement files and nostro statements, and exceptions that need ownership and an SLA.
One control before go-live: a back-valuation gate with maker-checker approval.
  • Any entry whose value date is earlier than the current business date needs a reason code and a second approver.
  • No entry may cross a sealed period.
  • Entries the close itself generates (fees, fee reversals, capitalised interest) are exempt, because they are derived and reproducible. Accrual deltas are memos, not ledger entries, so the gate never sees them.
  • All other back-valued entries feed a daily report reviewed by Finance and Compliance, showing customer impact (fees, interest) per entry.

In this core that means one check in _post plus an approved_by field on the entry. The record stays append-only, and the gate controls who may write it.

3. Authorization lifecycle

In this model an authorization has three states: APPROVED, DECLINED and SETTLED. Apart from a matching settlement it can end in only these ways. Not modelled, but needed before production:

  • Merchant void and timeout reversal. These release the hold immediately and must be idempotent.
  • Incremental and partial authorizations. A hotel extends the hold; a split shipment arrives as several captures.
  • The account or card is blocked or closed while a hold is active. The hold stays until its settlement or expiry, but no new authorizations are approved.

4. What I cut and why

SimplificationWhy it is in scope to cutProduction risk it defers
Single-sided entries per account, with no double entryThe brief asks for customer balances only.The books can't be proven to balance. Fee income, interest expense, suspense and the scheme settlement account have no contra entries. Double entry is the first thing to add.
No persistence, single processThe brief says in-memory only.A crash loses state. Production needs a durable write-ahead log before acknowledging any event, plus replay to rebuild. Downstream events need a transactional outbox, with the event row written in the same transaction as the entry, retried with capped exponential backoff and jitter. That is the design I shipped for Colourlab AI's trial events.
No concurrency controlThe stream is replayed serially.Two authorizations racing on the same account can both pass the available-balance check. This needs per-account serialization or optimistic versioning.
The close re-tests the whole windowSix days, two accounts.The quadratic close described in §1.
Stream order is the clock, with no late-arrival cut-offThe brief fixes the order.A statement goes out wrong, as with Day 5 for ACC-002, which the failing test shows. Production needs a cut-off time per booking day and correction notices.
No hold expiry, void, or incremental authorizationNot in the stream.Holds that never release, so customers see less available balance than they have (§3).
A flat 0.04 % per day with per-day roundingMatches the rule as written.No day-count convention or tiers. Rounding drift of up to ±0.005 per day (0.79 paid against an exact 0.802).
Fee in AED only, no FXThe brief prices only an AED fee.A non-AED account can't be charged. The close records an ERROR notice rather than guessing a rate.
Debits always post; no overdraft limitThe brief tests fees, not limits.A customer can be overdrawn without bound. Production needs a limit, and a decline path for debits.
Reversals are full only, and a reversal can't be reversedThe stream reverses one whole debit once.A partial refund or a corrected reversal can't be expressed. It needs partial reversals with a running reversed amount, and a rule for re-reversal.
No future-dated entriesThe stream has none.Scheduled payments can't be booked ahead. An entry whose value date is after the day it is processed is refused with an ERROR, not posted.
Days without a calendarThe window is abstract.Weekends, holidays and cut-off times change value dates and fee days.
No operator identity, reason codes or approvals on entriesNo users in scope.No audit of who back-valued what, and no maker-checker (§2).

The trade-off behind most of these cuts: I kept the parts the brief can falsify, which are replay order, the fee cascade, refunds, rounding and immutability, exact and tested. I documented the rest as deferred risk rather than half-building it.
